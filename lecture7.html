What is a Resource?

A resource is anything required to perform any activity in the project. In project management, resources fall into three main categories: Labor, Materials, and Equipment. Effective project scheduling must account for both the timing and availability of each resource type.

1. Labor (Human)
  • Salaried staff — Project manager, superintendent, project engineer, secretary; tied continuously to the project
  • Hourly workers — Hired for specific tasks: carpenters, masons, ironworkers, electricians, foremen
2. Materials
  • Installed materials — Stay permanently in the project: concrete, rebar, brick, mortar, insulation, framing wood
  • Construction materials — Used during construction only, e.g., formwork and scaffolding
3. Equipment
  • Construction equipment — Used during build, not permanently installed: bulldozers, backhoes, cranes
  • Installed equipment — Remains after completion: emergency generators in hospitals/industrial projects
// KEY INSIGHT — WHICH RESOURCES TO LEVEL? Not all resources need to be leveled. Resource leveling applies primarily to hired or rented resources (labor and major construction equipment), because the goal is to improve work efficiency and minimize cost. Permanently installed materials, for example, do not benefit from leveling.
Resource Allocation vs Resource Leveling
Resource Allocation

Assigning the required resources to each activity in the required amount and at the right time. It answers: how many of each resource does each activity need, and when?

Resource Leveling

A technique that minimizes period-by-period variations in resource loading by shifting tasks within their slack (float) allowances. The purpose is to create a smoother distribution of resource usage across the project timeline.

Two Objectives of Resource Leveling

1

Determine the resource requirements so that they will be available at the right time — avoiding sudden shortfalls or excess demand.

2

Allow each activity to be scheduled with the smoothest possible transition across usage levels — eliminating peaks and valleys.

Resource Conflicts occur when more resources (people or machines) are needed than are available — or when the same person/machine is required in two concurrent tasks. Leveling resolves these conflicts, usually at the expense of one of the triple constraints: time, cost, or scope.
Resource Leveling vs Resource Smoothing

These two techniques address resource conflicts differently. Understanding the distinction is critical for choosing the right approach.

# Resource Leveling Resource Smoothing
1 Resource-limited scheduling; importance is given to the limited resources Time-limited scheduling; importance is given to the duration of the project
2 Removes all resource conflicts Removes as many resource conflicts as possible — but may not remove all
3 May not require additional resources May require additional resources to address leftover conflicts
4 Activities may be shifted beyond their available float when rescheduling Activities are shifted only to the extent of the float available
5 Generally, the project duration gets extended The project duration remains the same
6 May change the critical path No change in critical path
Resource Leveling Steps
1
Create a project activity network diagram
Draw the precedence network (AON or AOA). Perform CPM calculations to find the critical path, early/late start and finish times, and total float for each activity.
2
Build a resource requirements table
Create a table listing each activity, its duration, the type and quantity of resources needed, and the total float available. Float tells you how much flexibility you have for shifting the activity.
3
Develop a time-phased resource loading table
Lay the activities across a day-by-day (or period-by-period) grid — initially scheduled at their Early Start times. Sum the resources needed per period to reveal the demand profile.
4
Identify conflicts and smooth using heuristics
Find periods where demand exceeds the available limit. Shift non-critical activities (using their float) to reduce peak demand. Re-check successors after every shift. Repeat until all conflicts are resolved.
Heuristic Rule: Always prefer shifting a non-critical activity before touching critical ones. Critical activities have zero float — shifting them directly extends the project duration.

Applying Resource Leveling

Resource leveling removes all conflicts, even if the project duration must extend.

①
Conflict on Days 6–9: Activities C and D overlap
C is on the critical path; D is not. Delay D until after C finishes (Day 10). D shifts to start on Day 11. Resource demand on days 6–9 drops to 5 compressors (only C). D has sufficient float to absorb this shift.
②
Successor F must also shift
Since F depends on D, it moves with D. After D finishes (Day 14), F starts Day 15.
③
New conflict on Days 15–16: E and F overlap (4+4 = 8 compressors)
E is on the critical path; F is not. Delay F until after E finishes (Day 19). F shifts to start Day 20. G must shift too, since G depends on F.
④
Final result
No more resource conflicts. Max compressors = 6 on any day. Project duration extended to 22 days. New critical path: A → B → C → E → F → G.
Trade-off: Resource leveling eliminated all conflicts but extended the project by 2 days (20 → 22 days) and changed the critical path. Activity F became critical.

Applying Resource Smoothing

Resource smoothing removes as many conflicts as possible without extending the 20-day project duration. Activities can only be shifted within their available float.

①
Delay D using its free float (FF = 1)
Shift D by 1 day (start Day 6). This partially reduces the conflict on days 6–9 but cannot eliminate it entirely — D still overlaps with C for 3 days (Days 6–8), still requiring 7 compressors.
②
Further delay D using total float (TF = 8)
By shifting D within its full float (up to 8 days), the conflict on Days 6–9 is resolved. However, since the project duration cannot move, activities can only shift to the extent their float allows.
③
Residual conflict on Days 15–16 remains
E (critical, 4 compressors) and F (shifted, 4 compressors) still overlap on days 15–16 since F cannot shift beyond its float without extending the project. This conflict cannot be fully resolved — 8 compressors still needed on those days.
Result: Project duration stays at 20 days. Critical path unchanged. Most conflicts on Days 6–9 are resolved, but Days 15–16 still exceed the limit (8 compressors needed). Resource smoothing cannot fully resolve all conflicts here.

Key Takeaways
Float is your lever

Non-critical activities carry float (slack). This float is the window for shifting them to reduce resource peaks. Critical activities have zero float — shifting them always extends the project.

Critical-path priority

Always try to delay a non-critical activity before touching the critical path. When resolving a conflict between a critical and non-critical activity, move the non-critical one.

Cascading effects

Shifting any activity may shift its successors. Always re-check all successor activities after each adjustment — a single delay can propagate through the network.

Leveling vs Smoothing

Choose leveling when resource limits are hard constraints. Choose smoothing when the deadline cannot slip. Smoothing may leave residual conflicts; leveling always resolves them.
